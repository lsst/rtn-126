\subsection{PSF vs CCD height chip}

\begin{figure*}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[scale=0.27]{dT_all}
        \includegraphics[scale=0.27]{heightMap_all}
        \caption{\small TO DO}
        \label{fig:dT_vs_height}
\end{figure*}

Before turning to a detailed characterization of how well the PSF model
reproduces the PSF, we first present a basic analysis of the PSF itself that
motivates most of the technical choices made for DP2, in particular the order
of the per-chip spatial interpolation. While the Rubin Observatory was still in
commissioning, one of the first things to stand out was that the spatial
variation of the PSF appeared to be optically dominated, and that the ``blob''
pattern seen in DP1 with the Commissioning Camera was still present in the ITL
chips of LSSTCam, but absent in the E2V chips. This blob was the justification
for using a fourth-order spatial interpolation in DP1 (\REF), and therefore in DP2.
This raises the question of why fourth order specifically, and what its physical
justification is. To answer it, we need to zoom out (Originally descriped in technoe \REF). 
In the left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:dT_vs_height}, we average $T$ across all single 
visits of DP2 in superpixels of $X\times X$; on each visit, and for a given CCD, 
we first remove that CCD's average $T$ (a simple way to remove the atmospheric seeing 
and focus on the optics). The right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:dT_vs_height} shows the height
variation of LSSTCam measured at SLAC \REF, with the average height of
each CCD removed. There is a clear correlation between the average PSF size and
the height map. On the ITL chips in particular, the height map shows a circular,
bent pattern (right panel), and this pattern is imprinted onto the PSF shape.
Such structure cannot be captured by a simple second-order polynomial
interpolation, which justifies moving to a higher order, as described in the DP1
paper. In short, the observed pattern reveals local focus variations across the
focal plane, caused by height variations of the CCD chips themselves.

\begin{figure*}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[scale=0.1]{z4_FoV_11_i}
        \includegraphics[scale=0.1]{z4_FoV_15_i}
        \caption{\small TO DO}
        \label{fig:z4_slices}
\end{figure*}


It is nonetheless surprising, and was not expected, to find the height map of
LSSTCam imprinted onto the shapes of stars (and galaxies). To understand the
physical origin of this effect, we need to look at the Active Optics System
(AOS) itself, which was still being commissioned throughout the DP2 period; a
full description can be found in \REF. The idea is to cross-correlate the
AOS parameters with the PSF and to look for a setting that minimizes the
correlation between the PSF second moments and the height map; such a minimum
should indicate that the camera is at least in focus. For this purpose we
collect the Zernike coefficients measured from the four corner wavefront
sensors, and we focus on the median value of $z_4$, which we use as a proxy for
the focus state of the camera. In Figure~\ref{fig:z4_slices} we reproduce the
left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:dT_vs_height}, but now for a single filter and within
a narrow slice of $z_4$: visits sharing a common $z_4$ should have a similar
defocus, which lets us average over different atmospheric seeing conditions. The
left panel shows the average $T$ in the $i$ band for $z_4 = 0 \pm 0.05~\mu$m ---
the mean and the value targeted by the AOS system --- and it correlates with the
height map (left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:dT_vs_height}). The right panel shows the
average $T$ in the $i$ band for $z_4 = -0.2 \pm 0.05~\mu$m; here the correlation
with the height map vanishes, indicating that this is close to the optimal focus
value. In short, the height map is imprinted onto object shapes in the DP2
dataset because the AOS target was slightly out of focus. This was corrected in
mid-December 2025.

\begin{figure}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[scale=0.2]{rho_vs_z4_per_filter}
        \caption{\small TO DO}
        \label{fig:z4_sweep}
\end{figure}


A natural follow-up question is whether the optimal target is the same in all
filters. To address this, we perform a statistical $z_4$ sweep: for a range of
$z_4$ values we compute the correlation coefficient between the PSF size and the
height map in each of the $u$, $g$, $r$, $i$, $z$, and $y$ bands. The results are
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:z4_sweep}. While $u$, $g$, $r$, $i$, and $z$ share the
same optimal focus point, the $y$ band appears to have a different optimum. This
is an empirical observation for which we do not yet have a definitive
explanation. The physical expectation is that $y$-band photons convert deeper in
the silicon, placing the focus at a different location relative to the mean
surface of the focal plane.

\begin{figure}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[scale=0.1]{focus_gradient_visit_2025042700505}
        \includegraphics[scale=0.1]{focus_gradient_visit_2026010100230}
        \caption{\small TO DO}
        \label{fig:gradient_examples}
\end{figure}

Two further questions arise from this analysis: does each wavefront sensor
require a different target offset, and can the approach be generalized to other
Zernike coefficients to reach a better optical state and improved image quality?
We first attempt to quantify the former. Since the start of commissioning, and
in particular since LSSTCam first went on sky, it has been clear that visible
optical gradients can appear across the focal plane; in extreme cases, stars
look like mini-donuts on one side of the focal plane and round on the other. We
quantify this using a cross-analysis with the height map, based on two
quantities. The first is the gradient of $z_4$ across the focal plane,
$\nabla z_4$: from the four wavefront sensors we compute the gradient of $z_4$
and record its amplitude $|\nabla z_4|$ and direction
$(\nabla_x z_4, \nabla_y z_4)$. The second is the gradient of $\rho$ across the
focal plane, $\nabla \rho$, where $\rho$ is the correlation coefficient between
star size and the focal-plane height map. We measure one value of $\rho$ per
science CCD, from which we compute the gradient $\nabla \rho$ and record its
amplitude $|\nabla \rho|$ and direction $(\nabla_x \rho, \nabla_y \rho)$. Our
hypothesis is that if $\nabla z_4$ and $\nabla \rho$ point in the same direction,
the focal plane is tilted and introduces an optical (focus) gradient across the
image; if they point in different directions, the focal plane is not tilted. As
an example, Figure~\ref{fig:gradient_examples} shows a visit from early
commissioning (visit 2025042700505) with a large optical gradient, where
$\nabla z_4$ and $\nabla \rho$ point in the same direction, and a more recent
visit (visit 2026010100230), closer to focus, where the two gradients point in
different directions.

\begin{figure}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[scale=0.2]{optical_gradient_0_z4_norm_time_series}
        \includegraphics[scale=0.2]{optical_gradient_1_rho_norm_time_series}
        \includegraphics[scale=0.2]{optical_gradient_2_scalar_product_time_series}
        \caption{\small TO DO}
        \label{fig:gradient_timeseries}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:gradient_timeseries} shows the time series of these amplitudes.
The amplitude $|\nabla z_4|$ clearly decreases with time, reflecting the ongoing
improvements on the AOS side. In contrast, $|\nabla \rho|$ remains roughly flat,
with a slight increase after mid-December. This is expected: each CCD has its own
optimal focus point, and the mid-December correction brought most of the central
CCDs into focus while the outer CCDs remained out of focus, which increases
$|\nabla \rho|$. The scalar product $\nabla z_4 \cdot \nabla \rho$ measures
whether the two gradients are aligned. From the beginning of commissioning the
two gradients point, on average, in the same direction. Their alignment weakens
with time, as desired, but it does not vanish: the gradients remain partially
colinear throughout the period, which indicates that the focal plane still has a
residual focus gradient, i.e.\ is slightly tilted. A later analysis of the best
image-quality (IQ) visits confirmed that each wavefront sensor has a different
optimal value; these average to the value shown in Figure~\ref{fig:z4_sweep} and
were pushed to the AOS targets in mid-2026, which significantly improved the IQ
and enabled the start of the LSST survey. For the other Zernike coefficients,
cross-correlation with the height map shows no evidence of a different optimal
target, as expected: the other optical aberrations are not expected to correlate
with height variations. However, the best-IQ analysis mentioned above does
indicate improved targets for the other Zernike coefficients, which also helped
improve the IQ and start the LSST survey.

A classical way to assess PSF performance is to average the second moments
across the focal plane over many visits and to look for any systematic,
spatially correlated structure that remains. Figure~\ref{fig:secondMoment_3panel_fov_all_bands_ugr} and Figure~\ref{fig:secondMoment_3panel_fov_all_bands_izy}
shows the average of $dT/T$, $de_1$, and $de_2$ in focal-plane coordinates, in
superpixels of $150\times150$ pixels, for each band. Visits with a high stellar
density are excluded from the computation of the average PSF second-moment
residuals, in order to avoid the background-subtraction problems discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:psf_sky_coord}. Specifically, all visits within $10^\circ$ of
the LMC, within $5^\circ$ of the SMC, or at Galactic latitude $|b| < 20^\circ$
are excluded from Figure~\ref{fig:secondMoment_3panel_fov_all_bands}. Each band
exhibits different features, which we comment on below.

Across all bands, there is an annulus pattern at the edge of the focal plane.
This annulus can be traced back to a background-subtraction problem: it is
visible both in the raw images, before any instrument signature removal (ISR),
and after ISR, before the PSF fitting (see Figure~\ref{fig:image_ring_ccd}). It
then propagates into the PSF second-moment residuals because the background both
biases the second-moment measurements at low signal-to-noise ratio and biases
the PSF model itself: by construction, the spatially varying background is
absorbed into the PSF model.

In the $u$ band, three distinct patterns stand out. The first is a rectangular
pattern that matches the CCD amplifier layout; this is also a background
problem. In early DRP processing of LSSTCam, this amplifier pattern was present
in the other bands as well, until a dedicated correction at the ISR level was
implemented (see \REF), which removed it from all other bands; it remains only
in the $u$ band, which is known to be more challenging. The second is a stronger
pattern on the ITL chips, similar to that shown in Figure~16 of the DP1 paper
(\REF). In the $u$ band, the polynomial interpolation is kept at second order,
because the stellar density is not sufficient to constrain a fourth-order
interpolation. The third is an annealing pattern, present only in the E2V
sensors, due to the coating of the CCDs (ADD MORE DETAIL LATER AND \REF). This
is also a background problem, visible in the images before they enter the PSF
fitting (see Figure~\ref{fig:image_annealing}).

In the $g$ band, the only band-specific pattern that stands out is the same
annealing pattern as in the $u$ band (see Figure~\ref{fig:image_annealing}). In
the $r$ and $i$ bands, nothing specific stands out beyond some ITL-chip
structure, which simply reflects how complex the chip bending is. In the $z$ and
$y$ bands, other patterns begin to appear. First, some fringing, which is also
visible in the image background before it enters the PSF model --- again a
background problem (see Figure~\ref{fig:image_fringing}). Second, more structure
becomes visible, especially on the ITL chips, such as the mid-line break (\REF).

Overall, all the remaining features are related, in one way or another, to
background subtraction. Paths forward for improving it are discussed in the
Discussion (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
